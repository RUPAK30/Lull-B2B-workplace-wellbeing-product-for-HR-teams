Safety and limits

What Lull does, and what it doesn't.

Lull suggests three-minute desk resets. It is not a medical device and can't see anyone. This page sets out how it handles pain, what it will never claim, and where reviews stand today, including the parts that aren't done yet.

Last reviewed October 2026 · Pilot-stage product

When someone mentions pain or symptoms

Lull checks for this before anything else, on the home screen and in the middle of a session. Safety always wins over any other request in the same message.

Urgent symptoms

For example: chest pain, trouble breathing, fainting, numbness, sudden or severe pain.

No session is offered. It says this needs urgent medical attention, not exercise, and asks the person to contact their local emergency number or urgent care now.

Pain, injury or feeling unwell

For example: "my back hurts", dizziness, a sprain, cramp, feeling sick.

It won't build a session around it, and suggests advice from a doctor or physiotherapist. Mid-session, the timer pauses, no more movements are offered, and the person decides whether to stop.

Recovering or in rehab

For example: "after surgery", "seeing a physio", "recovering from".

It says their clinician's advice comes first and that these sessions are not a rehab plan.

How it recognises this, and the honest limit. A fixed list of words and phrases always runs and can't be switched off. With the AI turned on, it also reads the message and can catch wording the list misses, like "my knee feels weird". The AI can only add caution, never remove it, and the safety wording people see is always Lull's own fixed text.

In our test of 44 realistic messages, the list alone missed 10 of 16 body concerns; the list plus the AI missed none. That is a small test, not proof. Either can miss unusual wording, and neither can judge how serious something is. This is a safety net, not an assessment.

With the AI on, typed messages are sent to Anthropic to be read. Taps and choices are not. Session cues ask people to move only in a range that feels comfortable, and to stop anything that doesn't feel right.

What it never claims

  • That anyone exercised. Timers measure time on a page. The end-of-session record says how long timers ran, not what someone did.
  • Weight loss or treatment. No promises about weight, pain relief, posture or health outcomes.
  • That it can see or hear you. Text only. No camera, no microphone, no motion sensing.
  • That you should keep a streak. No badges, no reminders you didn't ask for, no "you missed a day".

Content review

Every movement and cue was written to be gentle, low impact and doable at a desk or in a small space. They have not yet been reviewed by a physiotherapist. That review is required before any paid pilot.

Accessibility

The aim is WCAG 2.2 level AA.

Checked:

  • Text contrast meets AA in light and evening themes, measured for every text and background pair.
  • A whole session runs from the keyboard. Covered by an automated test.
  • Timer changes are announced to screen readers, and focus moves to each new step.
  • Reduced-motion settings are respected, and the layout works on phone screens.

Not done yet:

  • Testing with people who use screen readers day to day.
  • A formal audit by an accessibility specialist.
  • Languages other than English (Hindi is next).
  • Every reset has a seated version, but none has yet been reviewed with wheelchair users or people with limited mobility.
